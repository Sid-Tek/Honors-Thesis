DO THE boys and girls of Sweden play the
same games that we do in our country?
Do the children of Holland travel about
their country in vacation time? Is Norway
an exciting place in the winter time for
boys and girls? Is Switzerland anything
like the country Johanna Spyri describes
in "Heidi"? Would it be fun traveling
from the warm shores of France's Mediter-
ranean to the seacoast of Brittany? What
do the children of sunny Italy do to have
fun? These are some of the questions many
of the boys and girls of our own country
are asking.
There is a great and growing interest
among the children of America in the
children of the countries across the ocean
-in their work, their play, their countries'
geography, history and customs, their leg-
ends and folkways. Some young Ameri-
cans have had the privilege to visit these
countries abroad and to see them at first
hand. They are fortunate! But now mil-
lions of stay-at-home boys and girls can
read in Virginia Olcott's fascinating books
about the children of France, Holland,
Sweden, Switzerland, Norway and Italy
and enjoy the exciting and colorful ad-
ventures of Jean and Fanchon, Klaas and
Jansje, Erik and Britta, Anton and Trini,
Olaf and Ane and Beppo and Lucia, and
the wonderful times, at work and play,
in school and on vacation, they had to-
gether in their colorful homelands.

PREFACE
to the WORLD'S CHILDREN Series
EACH YEAR more people of the Western World set out
by ship and plane to visit the countries of the Old World.
Many of them are boys and girls, eager to see how their
Dutch, French, Italian, Swiss, Norwegian and Swedish cous-
ins live. But even if there were ten times as many ships and
planes available as there are now, only a relatively small
number of young Americans would be able to explore these
fascinating lands where Jean and Fanchon, Klaas and Jansje,
Beppo and Lucia, Olaf and Ane, Erik and Britta, or Anton
and Trini live.
Most American boys and girls still will have to be con-
tent to read about those countries they cannot visit and the
people who live in them. Many books already have been
written about them, but very few of those books make their
young readers feel so happily at home or so intimately fa-
miliar with the customs and daily living, the legends and all
the memorable sights and scenes to be found in each of these
faraway lands.
The author, Virginia Olcott, has lived in each one of the
countries about which she has written. She knows hundreds
of boys and girls in each of these countries, and she knows
the games they play, the clothes they wear, the homes in the
cities and villages where they live, and all the colorful
legends and lore of each country.

IN THE TULIP GARDEN
Jansje's Dutch tulips were dancing in the wind. Yes,
her tulips, hundreds of them, were bobbing up and
down and shaking their red, yellow, white, and pink
heads. They stood in neat rows on both sides of the
garden path. They bloomed under the farmhouse win-
dows. And the big, gay bed of colored tulips growing
in the grass plot looked like a patchwork quilt laid out
to dry.
"Jansje! Jansje!" someone called.
The Dutch farmhouse door flew open. Out sprang
Klaas Holkema, a boy with very long legs. The wind
ruffled his yellow hair till it stood up like a dandelion
blossom. His bright blue eyes peered round the garden.
"Jansje! Jansje!" called Klaas, as he ran along the
path. He was waving a bulging napkin with one hand.
"Jansje, where are you? See what I have! No, she is not
1
by the tulip bed," he sang. "She is not by the duck pond.
She must be in the barn."
Klaas Holkema was Jansje's cousin, ten years old. He
had been sick during the winter, and now that he was
better, was visiting Jansje. When school was closed for
the summer, Jansje was going with him to his dairy
farm. Klaas's farm was far away in Friesland, that part
of Holland lovely with quiet lakes, meadows, and busy
towns.
Jansje Koopman was eight years old, round, rosy, and
brown. She lived with her father and mother in the
farmhouse, near a little town in one of the flower-
garden islands in the Kingdom of Holland. These islands
are called Zeeland. How beautiful they are in spring
with gay tulips, sweet hyacinths, and other lovely
flowers! And Jansje's garden was the prettiest of all.
Jansje's town was very old. Three ancient gates led
into its narrow streets. Little gabled houses were there.
Two windmills were in the town. When the wind blew,
they turned their great arms round and round. Canals
instead of streets ran past the houses, with buttercups
and yellow irises bordering the quiet waters. Snow-
white ducks swam on the water, and brown-sailed boats
came gliding up the main canal from the river.
Father Koopman's farmhouse was called "Sunny
Corner." The name was painted in blue and yellow
letters over the door. How pretty the farmhouse looked
that spring afternoon with the tulips dancing around it!
So Klaas thought as he ran along, shouting for Jansje.
2
The sun shone on its rosy brick walls and danced on
the red tiled roof. The heavy wooden shutters and win-
dow frames, colored blue and yellow, looked as bright
as a paint box. A tall horse-chestnut tree on one side of
the path held up its blossoms, deep pink like burning
candles. The fresh curtains in the gabled windows
waved at Klaas. How happily the swallows darted about
the two squat chimneys! How gayly the cuckoos called
to him from the clump of young willows and maythorn
near the duck pond!
Klaas was calling, too, as he ran into the barn:
"Jansje! Jansje!"
There was a giggle from the hayloft, and Klaas
climbed up the ladder. A mound of hay moved. It
wriggled up and down. A round, rosy face with dancing
brown eyes peeped out of the mass of hay. Then Jansje
shook off the hay and sat up.
She spied the bulging napkin.
"What have you in that thing?" she asked.
Klaas poked the napkin longingly and laughed. Jansje
danced up and down like a gay little ball. She caught his
hand.
"Come," she cried, and both children scrambled down
the ladder and ran toward the duck pond.
"Maartje has been to town. She brought us these,"
Klaas said, as they sat under a shady maythorn bush.
Jansje watched him untie the corners of the mysterious
napkin.
"O-o-o-h! Oliebollen!” she exclaimed.

Yes, there they lay, two big fried sweet balls, as yellow
as gold and filled with plump raisins.
"I would rather eat my oliebol here than in the hot
kitchen," Klaas said, picking out the raisins one by one.
"So would the ducks!" Jansje pointed to the water.
Five white ducks were swimming across the pond. In a
moment they waddled out upon the bank and strutted
in a straight line toward the children. They flapped their
wings, craned their necks, and twitched their short,
feathery tails.
"They want supper!" Jansje broke tiny bits from her
pastry ball and scattered them on the grass. In a few
4
seconds the crumbs were gone and the birds were quack-
ing for more.
"They are greedy." Klaas tucked the last bite into his
mouth. "They swim about and catch flies all day. They
should not be so hungry."
"They should be like our tulips that drink sunshine
and eat sweet juices out of the earth," said Jansje. "See,
Klaas, see the tulips dance. They are having a party!"
she cried.
Yes, all the red and yellow and pink and white tulips
in the big flower bed were swaying from side to side and
tossing their heads. They looked as though they had
hold of hands and were dancing about.
But before Klaas could answer, a man's gruff voice
called from the barn, "Jumping Stick!"
"Whew!" cried Klaas. "I promised Long Jan to help
him milk."
And Klaas sprang to his feet and ran like a squirrel
toward the barn. Jansje picked up the napkin and fol-
lowed him.
LONG JAN
"Here I am, Long Jan," cried Klaas.
"So I see, Jumping Stick," growled Long Jan, the
farm hand, who was standing in the farm wagon. And
such a gay farm wagon it was, shaped like a big cradle,
its axles and wheels painted a bright blue.
6
Long Jan had just driven Jacobus, the plow horse,
up to the barn. The barn stood near the farmhouse. It
was built of rosy bricks like the house, but its roof was
thatched with straw the color of clouds before rain.
At one side of the barn were the shed for the chickens
and ducks, and the hayrick with its straw roof perched
on poles.
"Long Jan looks just like a funny stork," Jansje was
thinking as she ran after Klaas. She stood staring up at
the tall old man in the wagon. His face was gnarled and
tanned like leather. He stood looking down at the chil-
dren with his keen gray eyes. His grim mouth was
shut tight.
Yes, Long Jan did seem like a stork with very thin,
long legs. He wore the kind of clothes the country folks
in his own island wore. His island lay next to Jansje's.
He wore wooden shoes. He was dressed in a short, square
jacket, with a black vest and black trousers. About his
sunburned neck was a green scarf held together with
gold filigree buttons. On the front of his belt sparkled
two silver buttons as big as small saucers. And his shock
of gray hair was covered with a round felt hat.
Jan Loos helped Father Koopman on the farm. His
sister, old Maartje, helped Mother Koopman in the
house. And this is the way that Long Jan got his funny
name. When he was a boy, he and Maartje lived on the
next flowery island. On that island was a tower with a
tall, gold-colored steeple. It was a bell tower with forty-
one bells, which chimed some of the sweetest tunes in
7
all that country. How high the tower was! It rose above
the church, above the town hall, above the gabled
houses. It seemed to touch the clouds. People called it
Long Jan. And because Jan Loos was so big and tall,
they called him Long Jan too.
"Humph, Jumping Stick." Long Jan shook his fist at
Klaas. "It is nearly six o'clock. We need the Klopper
Man to tell boys the hour. But better late than never,
as the saying goes," added Long Jan as he climbed down
from the wagon. Long Jan had rheumatism in his long
legs.
8
Jansje laughed. "The Klopper Man will catch you,
Klaas," she cried. "I know all about him. Moeder told
me of the old Klopper Man who used, years ago, to walk
around town at night. He sounded his wooden klopper.
to tell the hour. The funny klopper is in our museum.
I'll show it to you.
"Museums may wait, Roly Poly," called back Long
Jan. "Now we must milk. It's late."
"I hear the carillon, Long Jan. You are wrong!
Listen!" Jansje cried.
Ring-a-ting-a-ling, sweetly the town bells were ring-
ing a little tune. Then louder bells struck the hour,
bim-bam, bim-bam! It was only five o'clock.
"As long as the sun works, we work," said Long Jan
grimly. "And the cows think so too. They are waiting
with their bags full of milk."
"Hear the cows. They are lowing," added Klaas, as
he unharnessed Jacobus and led him into his stall. The
children helped Long Jan drag the heavy wagon into its
place in the center of the barn.
Inside were the stalls and the stable for Jacobus.
Jansje stood still and looked around. There were the
plow, the reaper, the automobile. There was the high
tilt-cart which the family used to ride in before Father
Koopman bought the new automobile.
Jansje was proud of that automobile, but she liked
the tilt-cart better. It was so tall, that dear tilt-cart,
and its wheels and shafts were painted in lovely bright
colors.
9
"Now for the milk pail," said Long Jan.
Jansje patted his sleeve. "Let me help too," she begged.
"It doesn't take a whole mill to grind a peck of corn,”
grumbled Long Jan, handing Jansje the milk pail.

MILKING TIME
Klaas whistled as he carried the one-legged milking
stool over his shoulder. Jansje skipped along, swinging
the blue milk pail. Long Jan, his hands in his pockets,
a short pipe between his thin lips, walked ahead.
Down the roadway lined with lime trees the three
went, and out of the farm garden. The garden was
separated from the main road by a narrow canal full
of glittering water. A little bridge with a tall, brightly
painted gate crossed the quiet stream.
11
Klaas darted ahead to open the gate. The two children
rushed merrily across the road. There were no fences
anywhere, but a sloot-a ditch filled with water—ran
along the other side of the road. The children leaped
over the sloot and stood in the meadow waiting for
Long Jan.
"The field is turned to butter," Jansje cried, gazing
happily around.
Indeed, the broad, level meadow seemed scattered
with gold. Hundreds and hundreds of shining butter-
cups were nodding among the thick, high grass. The
meadow was separated from other green and blossoming
meadows by long, straight ditches instead of fences.
There were many fields neatly fenced in by ditches
wherever the children looked. In some, cows and sheep
and fat pigs were pastured. In others grew beans and
peas, red and white cabbages, cucumbers and carrots
and potatoes. Other fields were waving with wheat or
rye, barley or oats.
Eight black and white cows with their long-legged
calves were grazing in Father Koopman's meadow.
Splendid beasts they were, with soft, glossy bodies, big,
gentle eyes, and short, bent horns. Their backs were all
as straight as a ruler. Jansje skipped over to a handsome
cow and stroked her sturdy neck.
"Pretty, pretty Mina,” she said. "Long Jan, you must
milk this dear one first. See, she calls you."
Moo, moo! The big cow, switching her tail, slowly
lifted her head. All the other cows began lowing too.

Klaas knelt on the grass and tied the cow's hind legs
together with a piece of rope.
"Mina can't kick over the pail now," he said, knot-
ting the rope carefully. "Brother Bauke ties the hind
legs of all our cows when he milks. We have thirty cows.
I will tell you their names. Eke, Trientje, Ineke .

"Humph, Jumping Stick!" Long Jan tapped Klaas's
head. "This is not a cow's classroom. This is milking
time," and he sat down on the stool and commenced
milking. Steadily the stream of rich, foamy milk flowed
into the blue pail.
13
Several peasant farmers who helped Father Koopman
during the summer were milking too. Such a buzz of
sounds was there. Swish, swish, swish, swish, streamed
the milk into the metal pails. Moo, moo, lowed the cows.
Tweet, tweet, chirped the birds skimming above the
sloots. Baa, baa, answered the sheep that were grazing
in the next meadow. Cuckoo, cuckoo, sang the cuckoo
birds in the patch of trees a short distance away.
The children stood listening. They were also watch-
ing the two windmills in the town turn their long arms
in the wind.
Clack, clack, clack, the windmills groaned as they
turned faster and faster.
Then Klaas spoke.
"Long Jan, when you were a boy did you ever work
in a windmill?”
"Humph! I did many things. I fished, I sailed a boat,
and I worked for the miller, for I got up early,” an-
swered Long Jan. "Morning has gold in its mouth, as
the saying goes."
Klaas threw away the clover that he was nibbling.
"Tell us about it," he cried.
"Tell us," cried Jansje.
"I worked in a windmill like those over there. I helped
the miller carry sacks of grain up the ladders and dump
them into the bins, to be ground into flour. Nowadays
we have machines in our mills to haul up heavy loads―
but not when I was a boy. And nowadays," said Long
Jan, "many of our mills are run by motors. The millers
14
do not wait for the wind to turn their sails-but it was
not so when I was a boy."
"I love our windmills," sighed Jansje contentedly,
"especially the two in our town."
"Yes, love our windmills, Roly Poly." Long Jan
stopped milking. He looked over his shoulder at the
children solemnly. "They have helped to make our
country safe to live in. For hundreds of years windmills
have served Holland. The wind turns the sails. The sails
turn the machinery. The machinery works the pumps
to drain the water from the wet meadow lands, besides
grinding wheat for bread."
Then he added, "Much of our land is flat and hollow,
and the sea and rivers are higher than the land. So the
windmills, as well as the sea walls that we call dikes,
keep the water from overflowing our country. Do they
teach you that in school, young ones? Yes? Always re-
member it," said Long Jan gravely.
Klaas nodded thoughtfully. He could see a dike in
the distance. It ran straight and high like a raised road.
It was like a broad wall, protecting that part of the
meadow land which years before had been flooded by
water. But now the water was drained off, and in its
place lay beautiful polder meadows rich with vege-
tables and grain.
"Yes, I know why we have so many dikes," said Klaas.
"Some are only low banks. Others are high and built
with bricks and stones, like that one over there. And
many have grass and trees planted along their sides.
15
I like the ones with the trees best. The tops of the dikes
are nice to walk on."
"Do you have dikes near your farm in Friesland?”
asked Jansje. She had never seen Klaas's home. How she
longed to go there and see her uncle and aunt and
Klaas's big brother, Bauke; yes, and all the brothers and
sisters, ten of them. That would be fun!
"There is a long dike near our town," replied Klaas.
"Tall trees grow on it, and their branches stretch across
and touch one another. I ride along the top on my
bicycle. I think they must be the highest trees in the
world.

"I was a dike-mender once," said Long Jan, and went
on milking.
"What did you do?" Klaas asked eagerly.
"I helped to mend the dikes near the North Sea, that
great, gray sea. It rages over there on the other side of
this island. If it were not for the dikes and the sand hills
we call dunes, the North Sea would wash our Zeeland
Islands as clean as a scoured milk pan."
Long Jan stopped speaking. Swish, swish, steadily the
milk flowed into the pail.
"O-o-oh!" the children sighed in disappointment.
They wanted to hear more. Jansje lifted her brown eyes
and patted his knee with her little fat hand.
"Did you ever see a storm on the sea, Long Jan?"
"Terrible storms that roared like the Bad One,” an-
swered Long Jan and snapped his mouth shut. Swish,
swish, the milk shot into the pail.

LONG JAN SAVES THE DIKE
"I was born on the next island in a little fishing vil-
lage," began Long Jan. "But when I was a lad I went to
live in a village near the great North Sea. A lovely spot
that village is in summer time, when the sand dunes are
yellow with broom and the maythorn is white with
blossoms. Klic, klic, cries the gray windmill on the sea
dike. The red sails fastened to its long arms beat the
air. They turn the grindstone inside the mill, and the
wheat is ground to flour to make bread for the village
children.
"Often in summer the green sea sleeps. It lies quiet
under a soft blue sky. It lies quiet under the white,
fluffy clouds that look like sheep in a blue meadow.
17
The waves lap the foot of the sand hills that border
the coast. They sing a sleepy song. Gulls on silver wings
fly about. Sand birds peck among the shells on the shore.
"Then the boys and girls run to school, laughing and
shouting. Their wooden shoes call klop, klop on the
cobbled streets. Yes, the children wear wooden shoes in
that village. The little rosy-faced girls wear snowy,
hooded caps on their shining heads and big checked
aprons over their long black skirts. The boys look like
little men in their long trousers and tight black jackets.
How they race about the school yard at recess time!
They jump rope, play ball, and dance in circle games.
What a noise they make! What a noise their wooden
shoes make!
18
"Everyone works in that village. The mothers are
busy in their bright, painted cottages. They scrub the
floor. They scrub the outside walls and the doors. They
scrub the windows and stone steps. They scrub the cob-
bled streets. They fry flensjes, those crisp, thin pancakes
rolled in butter and sugar that the children love. And
they boil the fish that the fathers have caught in the sea.
They knit wool stockings for their families. They care
for the neat rows of vegetables in the tiny farm gardens.
Yes, the mothers are always busy.
"The fathers are busy too. They are fishermen and
dike-menders. In the summer they go out to sea in boats
and net for fish. And they mend the dike along the coast
-one of the largest dikes in our Holland.
"Pink-brown dunes stretch out on both sides of the
town. But just where the town is, there are no sand
dunes. The sea used to sweep over the land. Yes, a long
space used to be left open to the floods of the sea.
It is there that our great dike was built-a mighty sea
wall. And summer and winter the men watch that dike.
"In the winter how bitter the salt wind is! The sea
roars gray and awful against the dike. The clouds are
dull and black. The children race to school, klop, klop.
The boys hug their woolen coats. The girls tuck little
red hands under knitted shawls. How cold is the air that
blows from the sea! The winds howl with angry words.
They drown the klop, klop of the wooden shoes. They
drown the klic, klic of the mill. The mothers stay in the
cottages and keep the fires bright in the iron stoves.
19
They boil
pots of soup for the children. And when the
heavy storms come and the sea rises, the men go out to
watch the dike. For if the dike were to break, our village
would be flooded.
"This is what happened when I was a young man.
One winter day a frightful storm arose. Clouds black as
the darkest night covered the sky. The wind howled.
The waters of the sea dashed against the dike with
wicked strength. The women and the children stayed
in the houses.
"But the men, old and young, were on the shore. The
head man of the town had given each man a part of
the dike to watch. I was bidden to guard the end of the
dike near the windmill.
"To and fro I marched, watching the angry sea wash-
ing the dike's sides. The heavy waves thundered against
its stones. Up and down I walked. It was hard work,
but I was not there to catch flies, or to wipe up the sea
with a sponge, as the saying goes. My coat dripped with
rain. My wooden shoes were drowned in puddles. My
face was lashed by bitter winds.
"Suddenly I saw the wooden stakes at the water's edge
crash down. Then the waves hurled themselves with
frightful noise on the dike. The stone walls loosened.
Our dike was breaking!
"How I shouted! How fast I ran, waving my arms
and calling to the townsmen. They grasped their picks
and their heavy mallets. New stones and logs of trees
were ready waiting. Men and tools were at work in a
20
moment. We worked for many an hour. At last the hole
in the dike was stopped. Our work was done. The town
was safe.
"That night a grand feast was waiting in each cottage.

Smit, the shearer, who lived in town and went about

was right.
"People don't believe in kabouters any more," Klaas
called, as he walked around the flock of shorn sheep.
"And they don't believe in Saint Maarten's Night, but
they celebrate it in some of our towns in Friesland,” he

"What is Saint Maarten's Night?" Jansje looked up
in surprise.
Klaas tapped one of the sheep with his stick. The flock
was nestled together, bleating restlessly as they heard
their lambs baaing in the meadow. Klaas was waiting
for Long Jan to shear the last sheep; then all would be
driven back to the field.
"Saint Maarten's Night is on the eleventh of Novem-
ber," Klaas answered, with his eye on the flock.
"I once visited a boy. We and other boys and girls in
his village went about on Saint Maarten's Night. We
went from house to house, carrying paper lanterns and
lanterns made of turnips and beets. We carved funny,
grinning faces on the vegetables and put a lighted candle
inside each. We stood before the houses and sang this
song:
