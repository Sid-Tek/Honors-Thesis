PREFACE
to the WORLD'S CHILDREN Series
EACH YEAR more people of the Western World set out
by ship and plane to visit the countries of the Old World.
Many of them are boys and girls, eager to see how their
Dutch, French, Italian, Swiss, Norwegian and Swedish cous-
ins live. But even if there were ten times as many ships and
planes available as there are now, only a relatively small
number of young Americans would be able to explore these
fascinating lands where Jean and Fanchon, Klaas and Jansje,
Beppo and Lucia, Olaf and Ane, Erik and Britta, or Anton
and Trini live.
Most American boys and girls still will have to be con-
tent to read about those countries they cannot visit and the
people who live in them. Many books already have been
written about them, but very few of those books make their
young readers feel so happily at home or so intimately fa-
miliar with the customs and daily living, the legends and all
the memorable sights and scenes to be found in each of these
faraway lands.
The author, Virginia Olcott, has lived in each one of the
countries about which she has written. She knows hundreds
of boys and girls in each of these countries, and she knows
the games they play, the clothes they wear, the homes in the
cities and villages where they live, and all the colorful
legends and lore of each country.

FOREWORD
DEAR BOYS AND GIRLS:
Here I am visiting the children of France. In the
splendid city of Paris I have seen children playing in the
parks, racing along the quays, sailing boats in the foun-
tains; and I have watched Boy Scouts and Girl Guides
march with packs on their shoulders. Then there are
the boys and girls who have met me on the way in the
apple-blossom land of Normandy and the flowery coast
towns of the French Riviera. Today I am in Brittany
with the children of the sea.
In school, at work, at play, the bright, busy French
boys and girls remind me of my young friends in Amer-
ica. So I have written this story for you. You may enjoy
the adventures of Jean of fashionable Paris and his friend
Fanchon of the Riviera, on their picnics among the
olive-clad hills and at a country fair and on their trips
to the quaint markets. Go with them to see olive oil
pressed from the fruit, rich silks woven, and thousands
of sardines caught and canned for American children.
Help Jean and Fanchon to pick scarlet poppies and
climb the golden, gorse-covered Breton hills; then sail
away with the fisher folk of Brittany in a sky-blue boat
with yellow sails.
Go with Jean and Fanchon from the Riviera all the
way to Brittany, and you will learn how these World's
Children live, work, and play.

THE HOUSE OF THE GOLDEN MIMOSA
Fanchon ran into the garden of the Hotel Mimosa.
She stood peeping through the gate at the blue and green
waves of the Mediterranean Sea. The sun glinted on her
red beret and bag of school books. School was over for
the day.
"I shall pick Mimi a bouquet. She cannot talk, my
poor dear doll, but I know she loves flowers," she thought
to herself, and began to pick sprays of mimosa blossoms
from one of the trees.
Holding the golden flowers, she ran up the sanded
path to the hotel. Then she pranced into the hall where
her grandmother stood in the tiny office.

briskly ordering the maid-servant to make ready two

Fanchon, you must know, lived in a little town in
Southern France on the coast of the Mediterranean Sea.
All that lovely, flowery, fruitful coast is called the
French Riviera. The town lies on a flat strip of land
between the sea and a circle of olive-clad hills. Beyond
these hills stand the distant Alps, beautiful against the
sky and snow-crowned through much of the year.
Fanchon's parents had died when she was a baby, and
for eight years she had lived with her grandmother. She
was always excited when new guests visited Grand-
mother Fourcette's hotel. Americans, English, Germans,
Swedes, and people from many other lands came to en-
joy the wonderful sea air.
And Grandmother's house was called the Hotel

"His eyes are brown, just like chestnuts!" thought

"Françoise Marie Fourcette, Madame," answered
Fanchon shyly. She was ashamed that this handsome

"Madeleine Fourcette is my grandmother, Madame,"
replied Fanchon. "We live here in our Hotel Mimosa."
Then Grandmother Fourcette herself appeared. Her
arms were full of packages which she had brought from

By JEAN GERMAIN BONNARD, AGE TEN YEARS

graceful pitcher, or a delicate dish. These were set
