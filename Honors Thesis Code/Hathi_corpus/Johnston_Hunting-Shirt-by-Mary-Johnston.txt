THE time lay somewhere in the final quarter of
the eighteenth century. The place was King's Val-
ley, in the Allegheny Mountains in the region
south of the James, within a Virginia misty as to
westward boundaries; the season the just begin-
ning spring of the sugar sap and the emerging
bloodroot and the winter snow yet on the moun-
tain heads.
Hunting Shirt prepared to go to a wedding. He
bathed in Rapid Creek, javelining his body from
High Rock. It entered the deep pool. Icy the
water, strong and whirling! Hunting Shirt dived
and swam and floated. He cleansed his body. All
day yesterday he had hunted, returning at night-
fall to the house of his brother-in-law, returning
with the bear. When now he turned upon his
back and looked westward, he could see above the
naked trees along the top of Old Stormy the range

of cliffs called the Bear's Den. He had been up
there yesterday, he and that bear.
Again he impelled his body around the pool,
then drew it out upon the greening grass. The
sun glinting in each bead of water made for him a
kind of fairy mail. He had a towel of his sister's
weaving and he dried his body and then to set the
blood tingling raced up the hill and down again.
There was no runner like him as there was no
hunter like him in these parts, though they owned
good runners and hunters. But he could load his
flintlock as he ran, and he could read as he ran any
manner of sign in earth and bush.
He returned, warmed, to the hemlock and the
heap of clothing, that which he had discarded and
that which he would draw on in honor of the
wedding. An eagle rose out of the wood across
Rapid Creek, soaring afar into the morning blue.
Hunting Shirt stood marking it withdraw, with-
draw, withdraw. His naked body in the sun and
shade grew taut toward the eagle and its space.
It might have lifted, winged heels and winged cap,
and followed after.

His body stood six feet, the wonderful body of
a young man, in health and in full tide of all
wilderness activity. Rightly spare it was, rightly
sinewy, and yet it had glow and morning and song.
His hair grew in wavy locks, light in hue and fine;
his eyes were gray; his features cleanly cut, his
face rather long and all the bones set right. Under
all weathering the color was blond.
The eagle soared until he was a speck, an eye in
the blue. There was only the blue. Hunting Shirt,
sitting down, put on new moccasins, leather foot
piece and tongue and flaps that reached above the
ankle and were made close with a thong. Followed
the leggings of fine, supple leather. He rose and
drew on woollen undershirt and breeches and

a dim blue, cape and skirt adorned by a green
fringe, the belt likewise of this green dye of which
his sister had the secret. The hunting shirt, caped,
belted at the waist, reaching full of skirt to the
knees, became a strong young hunter, tall, full of
skill and litheness.

When he was not fighting he was scouting. When

DEEP summer possessed the old land of the Tanasi,
small, fragmented division of the Cherokee. Deep,
deep, deep summer, stately and powerful. The
wild plum bloomed no longer or bloomed in mem-
ory and foretaste in a delicate eternal recurrence.
But the golden plums hung from the boughs. The
birds were not nesting, but a fresh nation of birds
peopled the woods. The leaves grew green and
broad, the grasses were in feather. Creeping and
flying and four-footed worlds knew that it was
heyday. High arched the blue sky. When there
befell thunderstorms, with tearing light and over-
whelming sound, came after them the deep glint
of the sun and, seen from some height, the splen-
did rainbow.
