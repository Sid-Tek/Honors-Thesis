TEE POWELL drove west from Chicago into the
suburb of Hillport. It was the last day of May,
and the countryside had the look of early sum-
mer. Stee was to dine with friends in the suburb, and a
missed appointment had left him with time to kill.
His olive and maroon roadster reached the Hillport
station just as the 4:05 from Chicago arrived. Stee
could have driven under the elevated track and gone
on about his business. But as a city dweller and a
bachelor he was always amused by those heavily mar-
ried persons who infest suburbs. He drew up at the
curb.
Arrivals by the 4:05 were few. Two middle-aged
women carrying their purchases took possession of the
station taxi. A harassed man in an unpressed suit
hurried away on foot to mow his lawn. Two truants
from business got into a car parked with their golf
clubs in the back seat. Last of all came a girl in grey,
with a violet hat pulled smartly down to her eyebrows.
She must have come through from a distance, for
she carried a dressing case, and glanced about as if she
expected to be met. Stee, who saw blue prints and
drawings better than he saw at a distance, could not
7
make out her features. But he had an impression of
slimness and blondeness, and of an easy graceful swing
to her.
He was about to start his car when the girl came
walking up to him. "Have you been sent to meet me?"
she began. Then she shifted her dressing case and held
out her right hand. "Why, it's Stephen Powell! I
didn't know you at first without your glasses."
Stee felt a little thrill of pleasure as recognition
came to him. "I knew you without 'em. You're
Blanche Beacon."
"Right," said Blanche. She was used to being not
only recognized but identified. Her name meant some-
thing.
"Did you come out here to a house party?" asked
Stee. "We had a good time at that week end in the
Berkshires where I met you."
"I remember your idea of a good time. You dragged
me for a five mile walk to see the sun rise."
Stee chuckled. "Made you get up early for once."
"No. I fooled you. I stayed up. But I was more wel-
come in the Berkshires than I am here. They met my
train."
"Get in and I'll take you to your friend's house."
"Not a friend," said Blanche casually as he opened
the door for her. "Just a brother-in-law."
"He probably expected you to use the station taxi.
What's the address?"
"Some number on Fulton Avenue. The name is
Howard Hall."
"Fulton Avenue is one of the streets that lead down to
8
the river. I'll drive up River Street until we come to it."
The few shops clustered about the station. In two
blocks they were succeeded by lawns and trees, and
studiously picturesque houses. "A pretty place, isn't
it?" remarked Blanche in some surprise.
"Is this your first visit?"
"Howard built out here only last year," Blanche
explained. "He's my sister's widower, and for some
reason he hasn't remarried. He's always writing me to
come out and pay my nieces a visit. The last two
summers I've been in stock on the Coast. But this
summer I'm free, so I thought I'd take him at his
word."
"Are you resting in a professional sense?"
"No. I have my contract signed. I'm to begin re-
hearsals in August."
Stee chuckled. "I'm curious to see what you make
of Hillport."
Blanche dimpled. "It looks like a good place to
settle down."
"You get it. Settle down and be settled."
"The houses are lovely, anyhow. Aren't you an
architect? Perhaps you built some of these houses
yourself. Show me which ones."
"Not one, as it happens. I specialize on apartment
buildings. There's more money in 'em. Anyhow if you
build a house you always have to satisfy some woman.
It's hard work satisfying a woman."
Blanche shot him a look from very blue eyes dark-
ened effectively by the tiny brim of her violet hat.
"Did you ever try?"
9
"I've learned from the experience of my friends.
How long do you expect to be here?"
"Howard wants me to make a long visit. His two
girls are getting into their teens, and he wants a re-
fining influence turned on them."
"Does he think you can turn it?"
"Why not? I'm their aunt. And I'm only ten years
older than the elder of them. If I find I like it here, I
shall stay all summer."
"You'll do well if you stay a week," snorted Stee.
"You say that just to be contrary. It looks quiet and
pleasant here. The golf ought to be good, and I be-
lieve Howard has a small car that I can drive."
"You can drive with your car full of women. You
can play golf with women. You will play bridge with
women."
Blanche was dismayed. "Aren't there any men in
Hillport?"
"Not the kind of men you're used to. Other wom-
en's husbands, perhaps. But they wouldn't be inter-
ested in you. All the interest of their lives centers in
Chicago. They go in to business every morning. They
stay in town if they want amusement. They come
back here to sleep."
"But what on earth do the women do?"
"Go into Chicago themselves if they can hunt up an
excuse."
"And if they can't?"
"Then they hang around each other's houses and
see what mischief they can get into."
Blanche laughed. "Gad, what a life! And that goes
IO
on year after year?"
"Perhaps they like it. After all most women
marry for security, and you can't have security with-
out sameness."
"Well, I'm not married to it, you know," said
Blanche coolly. "If I don't like the life I shan't stay.
What a shallow river! Is all Hillport on this side of
it?"
"The opposite bank hasn't been developed yet. It
will be in time, but at present it's rather inaccessible."
Blanche glanced down another cross street toward
the river. "It looks attractive. Fields and woods.
Rather flat, like all Illinois. Still it's country."
Stee hesitated a second before he made the plunge.
"It happens I thought of driving over there this after-
noon. I was on my way there when I met you. Are you
in any hurry?"
"Apparently not. At any rate my relatives are in no
hurry to see me."
Stee turned down the next cross street, and drummed
over a wooden bridge with insecure planking. "This
is the only way to cross," he said. "This house to our
right is the only one on this side of the river."
Stee was stopping in front of a small house of yel-
lowish brick, set so close to the ground that a single
step gave entrance. Its modest yard ran directly down
to the river, and was surrounded by a low brick wall
overgrown with climbing roses. The house was half
embowered in lilacs, some of which had not yet given
over blooming. But the windows were blank, the
grass uncut, the paths growing up to weeds.
II
"There isn't anyone living here," remarked Blanche.
"I wonder what has become of the old boy,"
mused Stee. "I haven't given him any work for some
time, but I never heard that he had left here."
"Who was he?"
"An Italian by the name of Signorelli. He made
terra cottas, fireplaces and benches and such things
that architects use. He did his work right here on the
premises."
"There isn't a sign of a terra cotta here now."
"There isn't a card either to tell where he's gone. I
wonder if Elliot Farwell would know."
"Who's Elliot Farwell?"
"A brother architect who lives in Hillport. I'm
supposed to have dinner at his house tonight."
Stee turned his car, and drummed again over the
bridge. Blanche leaned out to look back at the house.
"There's always a fascination about a deserted house.
I'm especially keen for that one."
"It is picturesque."
"And it's so isolated. I've sometimes thought I'd
like to take an isolated house and live there all by
myself."
Stee grinned. "I can just see you doing it."
"Maybe I won't do it. But it's a fascinating idea,
being a hermit."
"If you ever do turn hermit, come to me and I'll
give you some pointers. I'm the best little hermit you
ever saw."
"Go easy, Stee. You live in a city apartment like
anyone else."
12
"I have an apartment to sleep in, just as I have an
office to work in. But I have a lake place up in Wis-
consin where I do most of my living. Read and swim
and grouch. Do my own cooking."
"No women around to pester you?" suggested
Blanche.
"No one at all around. I own a hundred acres."
"I've been in Wisconsin," said Blanche. "I've acted
in both Milwaukee and Madison."
"Madison isn't so bad," said Stee. "But to appre-
ciate Wisconsin you must know the lake country. The
lake country of Wisconsin is a delight to the eye, the
ear, and the nostril, and only four hours by motor
from my office. Of course many of the lakes are dis-
figured by ugly shacks and contemptible hotels, and
blotted by the kind of people who do blot a landscape.
But I own a hill wooded with cedar, on the shore of
the most limpid lake outside a poem."
His sudden enthusiasm made a different man of him.
Blanche looked at him with deepened interest as she
asked, "Is there a house on your place?"
"Three rooms and a porch. It's built of cedar logs.
I did most of the work on it myself. I even cut down
some of the trees."
"That sounds fascinating. Whether I decide to her-
mit or not, I should like to see such a place."
Stee didn't want to be flattered by her interest, but
he was. "It might be arranged. If you're here for some
time, we could get up a party and week end there."
Blanche's eyes sparkled. "I should love that."
"Would you really?"
13
"Indeed I would."
Stee looked at her for a long minute, and then ab-
ruptly came to earth. "Damn it! I've turned west, and
Fulton Avenue is the other way."
"Never mind. Turn around."
"I'll keep on this way and let you see where I'm
dining tonight. Elliot Farwell lives on this street.
He's almost a neighbor of yours."
"Is he the sort of man you were speaking of, who
resides in a suburb but really lives in Chicago?"
"Lord, no!" said Stee in disgust. "I think he counts
the hours he has to be away from Hillport. Since he
married this girl he hasn't been able to see anything
but her. Another case of a good man ruined."
Blanche dimpled. "That's always the verdict of the
men friends. 'We like Bill but we can't stand his
wife.'"
"I don't dislike her. I suppose it's her job to make
Farwell happy. But her whole life is relative to his.
She never mixes with outsiders, and she has no inter-
ests of her own. She spends the day waiting for Far-
well to come home from the office and hold her hand."
"You're just jealous," said Blanche. "At the bot-
tom of their hearts that's the kind of woman all men
prefer. Just as they prefer a woman to have long hair,
though they may be afraid to say so."
"Your hair is curly, isn't it?" asked Stee, peeping
below the close hat-brim.
"What's left of it is. It is cut just as short as hair
can be cut," said Blanche serenely.
Stee stopped the car in front of a well proportioned
14
house of white stucco set in a fine sweep of lawn, with
another wide lawn at the back, terminating in a row
of Lombardy poplars. "That's Farwell's place. He
planned it himself, of course, and I must say he didn't
do badly."
"How long has he lived here?" asked Blanche.
"I should say about four years. He was married the
last year of the war, but he lived in town at first."
"Then he must have built the house to suit the
trees. Such trees don't grow in four years."
"Nor in ten. Oh, damn!"
"What's the matter?"
"There's Mrs. Farwell now. She's coming out.
We'll have to stop and speak with her."
"Why not?" said Blanche coolly. "It will give me
a chance to meet one of these suburban married women
you rave about."
Down the walk toward them came a slender slow
moving woman in a supple black dress. Her hair was
long, and worn in a simple knot at the back of her
head. She offered Stee a long slim hand with highly
polished nails. "Come in," she said with a slight smile
that included Blanche. "Elliott will be at home soon."
"I'll be back later," answered Stee. "At present I've
got to drive on and deliver Miss Beacon at her brother-
in-law's. Perhaps you know her brother-in-law. What
did you say his name is, Blanche?"
"Howard Hall. I'm spending the summer with
him."
"I have heard Elliot speak of a Mr. Hall whose
house he built. On Fulton Avenue, is it not?"
15
"It is," said Stee. "I got mixed and drove a block
too far before I turned. We were down looking for
old Signorelli, who used to have his workshop in
that little house across the river. What's become of
him?"
"Oh, he's dead! He died last fall."
"I hadn't heard," said Stee. "He was an interest-
ing old boy. Left no family, did he?"
"He had some relatives in Italy. They ordered every-
thing sold. My husband bought the place as it stood.
Now he has used most of the terra cottas, he finds
himself with the house on his hands."
"He ought to have no difficulty in renting it. It
would bring twice the rent though if it were over
here in civilization."
"I should like to live there myself. I find it quaint.
But like most Americans my husband prefers to live in
a new house."
"He prefers to live in a house of his own design-
ing."
"Oh, to be sure! For what you call the advertise-
ment." Again Mrs. Farwell's smile included Blanche.
This time it was the smile of one woman to another.
"Look Miss Beacon up, and have her meet some
women," commanded Stee. "Her brother-in-law may
not think to do it."
"I know very few people here," said Mrs. Farwell.
"But I shall certainly look her up. You and she might
come in now. When Elliot comes he mixes cock-
tails. He mixes them very well."
Stee looked hopefully at Blanche, but Blanche
16
shook her head. "I'm afraid not. I've got to get on
to the Hall's. They don't even know I've reached
town."
"Then I will look you up," said Mrs. Farwell. She
stepped back, and Stee started the car. At the corner
Blanche glanced around. Mrs. Farwell was still stand-
ing at the curb. She waved a hand at them.
"Stee, she's lovely!" said Blanche. "Her voice is
like honey. And that faint trace of a foreign accent:
it's like honey flavored with cinnamon. French, isn't
she?"
"She was a saleswoman in a dress shop when Far-
well married her. But I guess her accent is real enough.
Anyhow she never forgets it."
"She uses a lipstick but no rouge," commented
Blanche. "That's a good dress but it isn't new. A nice
black dinner gown lasts forever. I should say her
pearls are genuine."
"Probably they are. Farwell would be just idiot
enough to put a year's earnings into a necklace for her
to lose somewhere."
"What is Mr. Farwell like?"
"He's a prince of a chap, and an excellent architect.
Not such a good business man as I am, but wonderful
on the creative end."
"I mean, what does he look like?"
"Of course, you would mean that."
"Having got in a dig at me, suppose you answer my
question."
"Oh, he's tallish, and slender, and his hair is
getting grey. He has what on the stage you call dis-
17
tinction. You would look at him twice if you saw
him anywhere."
"They must make a handsome couple, Stee. Have
they any children?"
"No children."
"Do you see much of them?"
"As much as any outsider sees. I come out to their
house for dinner as often as he can get her to ask me.
That isn't often."
Blanche laughed. "I believe you're jealous. Mr.
Farwell is too happy in his married life to suit an old
bachelor like you."
"An old bachelor? I'm thirty-four by the calendar,
if you must know."
"Thirty-four is pretty old. At your age you should
have a wife of your own, and a home of your own."
"Nothing to it," retorted Stee. "My life isn't ex-
actly rapturous, but at least it's secure. A reasonably
unhappy marriage would be even more secure. But
when I see a man living in such a fool's Paradise as
Farwell, I tremble for his future. One false step and
his life would be shattered."
Blanche's eyes sparkled. "That's the worst non-
sense I ever heard. You're just jealous."

THEN Stee dined with the Farwells, he usually
left for home about eleven. This time he ac-
cepted their invitation to spend the night.
He and Farwell got into a long discussion of coopera-
tive apartments and garden cities, while Anne sat by
in smiling silence.
They had dined in a small glassed porch that looked
out toward the wide lawn and the Lombardy poplars,
darkly outlined against the night sky. Tall yellow
candles in green glass holders shone down on a green
glass bowl filled with jonquils and narcissus. A tall
green bottle of cordial made its appearance with the
cigarettes. A woman who was hired by the day
changed the plates and cleared the table.
It was a charming setting. Stee remembered his own
solitary town apartment, where he usually appeared
just in time to go to bed. He imagined himself in a
house like this, with a woman like Anne Farwell to
arrange flowers and light tall candles for him. But
his imaginary wife suddenly developed curly fair hair,
cut as short as hair could be cut. Stee grinned in self-
derision. "I can imagine a chippy like that shutting
herself up in Hillport to wait on my comings and go-
19
ings," he thought. "I wonder what made me tell her
my age. Thirty-four must seem ancient to her. Per-
haps though I might interest her by my mere an-
tiquity."
His vision of Blanche against an alien background
did not keep Stee from sleeping soundly in the Far-
wells' little guest room. But he was the first person in
the house to wake the next morning.
It has been a warm night, and all the bedroom doors
were left open. As he lay blinking in the early sun-
light, Stee could look down the hall into the front
room, where a little lacquered desk and a chaise
longue cushioned with cretonne were outlined against
the windows. Mrs. Farwell's bedroom doubtless
served her as sitting room too.
For a moment Stee's view of her windows was cut
off by a flash of orchid crepe, with a cloud of unbound
dark hair above it. It disappeared into the room oppo-
site the head of the stairs. In the stillness of the early
hour Stee heard a light rapid kiss, the murmur of
Farwell's sleepy voice, then a second kiss, a man's this
time. Stee winced and then grinned. That was why
married couples occupied separate bedrooms. It made
every morning a glad reunion.
Stee got up and shut his door. He heard the shower
bath turned on presently. Ten minutes after it ceased
he heard Mrs. Farwell go downstairs. By that time
Farwell was singing under the shower. But you didn't
have to be married to sing in your bath. When Stee's
turn came he fairly shouted.
The little orange and blue breakfast porch was
20
aglow with morning light. The grapefruit was ice
cold, the coffee hot, the toast crisp. Mrs. Farwell was
dressed for walking.
"I usually take Elliot to the station in bad weather,
or walk down with him when the day is fine," she
explained. "Today I suppose you will drive him into
Chicago. That cheats me out of my outing."
Farwell looked disturbed. "Why don't you run into
Chicago with us?" he suggested. "Haven't you some
shopping to do, or a matinée you want to see?"
"It seems like a great deal of bother, just to amuse
oneself."
"Why don't you pick up Miss Beacon and make her
go walking with you?" suggested Stee.
Farwell was interested. "Anne tells me you stopped
here yesterday with a most attractive girl, Stee."
"A nice girl," agreed Stee. "Belongs on the stage,
and knows her way about."
"Then do look her up, Anne," urged Farwell.
"Most of these Hillport wives don't appeal to you.
I can understand that readily. But you don't get out
enough. You ought to see more of people."
"I don't need many people," answered Mrs. Far-
well, pouring him a second cup of coffee. "However
if you want me to look Miss Beacon up, I've no ob-
jection."
"She won't be out of bed at this hour," said Stee
with a grin. "But if you dropped in on her about
eleven o'clock, you'd probably be a godsend. She says
she's going to stay in Hillport all summer, but I'm
betting she won't stay a week."

"If you're really interested in her," said Farwell,
"it would pay you to have Anne cultivate her."
"I'm only casually interested in her," said Stee.
"But she would make a nice friend for Anne."
"Anne is not conscious of needing any new friends,"
said that lady with a smile. "But she will certainly
go to see the beautiful blonde child. How old is she,
by the way? American women have a way of looking
so very young, and behaving as if they were very

"She's about twenty-five," said Stee. "She looks
eighteen, but that's a part of her stock in trade."
Immediately after breakfast Stee and Farwell drove
into Chicago. Stee found a contractor waiting for him
in his office, and by eleven o'clock he had forgotten
Hillport and its interesting inhabitants.
But punctually at eleven Blanche received the mes-
sage that a lady was downstairs asking for her.
Blanche hurried through the rest of her dressing; up to
that moment she had been killing time.
In a corner of the Halls' great living room Mrs. Far-
well was waiting for her. "I'd hoped to find you at
leisure," she explained as Blanche shook hands. It's a
beautiful morning for a walk, if you like to walk."
"I like to walk, but I don't like to walk alone,"
said Blanche.
They set out together down the elm shaded street.
"How few people one sees about at this hour!'' com-
mented Blanche. "What do all the women in Hillport
do with themselves?"
"They play bridge," said Mrs. Farwell.
22
"Not at eleven o'clock in the morning, surely?"
"In the morning they telephone to each other to
arrange bridge for the afternoon and evening."
"You speak as if you didn't play," said Blanche.
"In self defense I've learned to play. But it was a
mistake. I should have held to my ignorance."
"Don't you care for Hillport?"
"I sometimes think I should like to go farther out.
That is why I have thought of moving into that house
beyond the river that you saw yesterday."
"Can't you bring your husband to see it as you
do?"
Mrs. Farwell smiled. "I go slowly, not to hurt his
feelings. He planned our present house as a home for
me."
"Let's go down and have a look at that old house
now," suggested Blanche. "There's no harm in keep-
ing your eye on it."
They turned from Fulton Avenue into River Street.
"The river in itself is a disappointment," said Blanche.
"It looks like mosquitos."
"It breeds them," said Mrs. Farwell. "But there
would be no more on the opposite bank than here in
the town."
They walked along toward the bridge, catching
sight of the river between the houses. "There's the
old place now!" cried Blanche. "It makes a woman's
mouth water, doesn't it?"
"Yes. It is so mellow. I don't despair of living there
yet."
But as they stepped on the rickety bridge Blanche
23
gave an exclamation of dismay. "I believe someone is
moving in!''
"Surely not!" said Mrs. Farwell. "Who could move
in?"
"There is the van at the door now."
"There is a van, surely. How odd! I didn't know the
house was rented."
Blanche walked to the rail of the bridge. "Let's take
an eyeful and see whether we think the tenant is
worthy of the house. It would be a pity for that place
to fall into the hands of anyone who couldn't appre-
ciate it."
Blanche leaned on the rail and watched the "mov-
ers." "That's almost the last of the load," she said.
"They have to walk to the front of the van now.
Shabby looking stuff, isn't it? I'm not well im-
pressed."
"All goods look curious on a moving van," said
Mrs. Farwell.
The movers lifted down a small desk, and deposited
it on the path just outside the wall. At the same
moment a woman appeared in the doorway of the
house. For the time Blanche lost all interest in the
household goods. She stared in absorption as the
stranger advanced halfway down the path and then
stopped.
She was a woman sunk in apathetic middle age.
She wore a shapeless black dress gone green with
time, fastened at the neck with an old garnet brooch.
Her feet were thrust into heelless slippers that scuffed
as she walked. Her grey hair was untidy, and on one
24
cheek she was disfigured by a large brown mole.
It was hard to believe she belonged to the same
species as the two women who stood watching on the
bridge. Yet Blanche's very first glance made a star-
tling discovery. The strange woman might have been
some faded elder sister or neglected aunt of the woman
at Blanche's side.
Blanche turned around to verify the impression.
Mrs. Farwell was intent on the desk, which the
movers were now shouldering. The woman scuffed
back to the house and disappeared. Blanche put
her finger on the resemblance. It was a matter of
nationality.
"That woman looks French," she said crisply.
"I scarcely saw the woman," answered Mrs. Far-
well. "I was fascinated by that desk."
"Is it French too?"
"French, and very old. I have not seen one like
that since-oh, for many years!"
"You shouldn't have missed the woman," said
Blanche. "She was a sight. Yet if you'll pardon me for
saying so, in the drollest way she looked a little like
you."
Mrs. Farwell smiled. "Then I'm glad I missed her.
The people who are pointed out as one's doubles are
always such a blow to one's vanity."
"She wasn't in any sense your double. It was just
that she too looked French."
"You think I look French?"
"You are, aren't you?"
"I am. But as for looking so, it takes the eye of an
25
outsider to see nationality. I remember an American
whom I knew in Paris when I was a little girl. He
had lived in Paris for fifteen years, had all his clothes
made there, spoke the language like a native. But he
could never go near the Boulevards without being ap-
proached by at least one guide. To their eyes it was
plain at a glance that he was either American or
English. Yet he himself could not see how they
knew."
The movers had landed the desk in the house. They
came back to the wagon, clambered on it, and passed
the two women on the bridge. "That's all there is to
see," said Blanche.
Mrs. Farwell looked wistfully at the little house
embowered in lilacs, and then turned resolutely away.
"I hope they treat my house kindly," she said.
The two women returned up River Street. "One of
these days you might go to see how she is treating
your house," suggested Blanche.
"I think I shall spare myself that."
"But you realy might go to call on her," urged
Blanche. "If she has just left her own country, think
what it would mean to her to see a countrywoman
and hear her own beautiful language."
"But on my own account I don't wish to go."
"You think it might make you homesick for
France?"
"On the contrary, I don't wish to be reminded."
"That's odd," said Blanche bluntly. "Most French
people think they're in exile if they have to live
outside France."
26
"Then I'm the exception. But you see America has
